Alpine Plants Guide for Dogs

Some Alpine plants can make a dog seriously ill if chewed or swallowed. Use this guide to check which encounters need caution, prevent ingestion and respond promptly to a suspected exposure.

● Editorial review draft
!

If your dog may have eaten a plant

Move your dog away and prevent further exposure. Call a veterinarian or emergency clinic promptly, even if your dog has no symptoms yet. If the plant has red status, say so when you call and treat the situation as urgent.

  1. Photograph the whole plant, leaves, flowers or fruit, and where it was growing.
  2. Note the time, likely plant part and possible amount.
  3. Bring a sample only if it is safe and legal to collect; use gloves for unknown plants.

Do not induce vomiting or give food, milk, charcoal or medication unless a veterinary professional tells you to.

Identification limits and editorial status

This is awareness guidance, not an identification guarantee or a substitute for veterinary care. Appearance changes through the season, and similar-looking species can have very different risks. The catalogue is not exhaustive.

All 13 records are part of this editorial-review draft and are not presented as approved by a veterinarian or regional botanist. The page remains visibly labelled as a draft, and no unverified plant photographs are shown.

Method, sources and leave-no-trace

Toxicity classifications and emergency actions are tied to the source links shown on each card. Trail habitat, season and identification claims require regional botanical review. Leave wild plants in place: local protection rules vary, and a photo is safer and more useful than picking.
