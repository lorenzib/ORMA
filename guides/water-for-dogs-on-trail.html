Safety library / Water & hydration

Water for dogs on alpine trails

Your dog can’t tell you they’re two litres short. Work out what to carry and plan around real fountains.

2 min guide

In 20 seconds

  • On a hot hike, plan for 2 to 4 times your dog’s normal daily water. For a 25 kg dog that’s about 3 to 6 litres.
  • Offer small drinks every 15–30 minutes, before your dog asks.
  • Plan around fountains and rifugio taps, not streams.

Dogs cool down by panting, and panting uses up water. On an exposed Dolomites trail in July, your dog loses more water than you’d think.

Plan ahead

How much water

Working out what to carry60 ml per kilo at rest, an example, and a bit extra

The usual vet guideline is about one ounce of water per pound of body weight per day, or roughly 60 ml per kilo. That’s at rest. With long exercise, and above all in heat, a dog can need two to four times as much. A 25 kg dog needs about 1.5 litres on a lazy day at home. On a hot, shadeless 4-hour hike, plan for 3 to 6 litres, counting what you carry and what you can refill on the way.

Offer water every 15–30 minutes while you walk. Small drinks often are better than one big one. If your dog is panting hard, offer water before they ask. At altitude the air is drier and the sun stronger than the temperature suggests, so plan for the extra even on days that feel mild.

Then carry a bit more than that. Bring a folding bowl. Take more on exposed routes, and more again for flat-faced or heavy-coated dogs, who can overheat sooner. Our safety guide covers which builds need extra care, and the guide to lifts can help you skip an exposed climb.

Read your dog

Signs of dehydration

Four checks: gums, skin, eyes, behaviourWhat to look for
CheckWhat to look for
GumsThey should be wet and slippery. Dry or sticky gums are an early sign.
Skin tentGently lift the skin near the shoulder blades and let go. It should snap straight back. If it settles slowly, your dog has lost fluid.
EyesSunken eyes come later and are more serious.
BehaviourTiredness, slowing on flat ground, or panting harder than the pace explains. These are the same early signs as heat stress, because the two come together.

If you see these, stop in the shade, rest and give small amounts of water. A dehydrated dog that gulps a whole bowl can bring it straight back up, which makes things worse. If the signs don’t clear quickly, go to a vet. Don’t wait and see.

Water points

Finding water on the route

ORMA maps 16,581 drinking-water points across the Dolomites and Savoy, from village fountains to trailhead taps and springs. You can switch them on as a layer on our map.

Every trail page lists the water points on that route, with the kilometre for each one. Before you start, find the longest dry stretch and carry enough to cover it.

Check the list against the day’s heat before you set off. What matters is the longest gap between points, not how many points there are.

Fountains can be seasonal. Walkers report dry fountains on ORMA, and the reports show on the trail page with who sent them and when.

Streams, lakes and troughs. Cattle and sheep graze above a great many alpine streams, and standing water can carry parasites like giardia. A drink from a fast, cold stream is usually fine if you’re stuck, but don’t count on one. Skip cattle troughs. They’re standing water with livestock around them.
